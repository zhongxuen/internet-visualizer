# Firewalls and segmentation

A <Term>firewall</Term> is a policy applied at a boundary. Almost everything interesting
about one is the choice of boundary, not the choice of rules — a perfect ruleset in the
wrong place protects nothing, and a crude one in the right place protects a great deal.

## Why the boundary is where it is

That office has one switch and three <Term id="vlan">VLANs</Term> on it — staff, guests,
and servers. Traffic within a VLAN never leaves layer 2 and no firewall can see it.
Traffic between VLANs must go through a layer-3 device, and that forced detour is the
entire point: it creates a place to stand.

Segmentation is what turns one compromised laptop into a contained problem instead of a
company-wide one. The guest network is the clearest case — it needs the Internet and it
needs nothing else on the premises, and putting it in its own VLAN says so structurally
rather than hopefully.

## Stateful, and the two ways to say no

A stateful firewall remembers connections it allowed outward so it can let the replies
back in. That is what makes "allow outbound, deny inbound" a workable default instead of
a description of a network nobody can use — and it is the same table idea as

<Term>NAT</Term>, doing a different job.

When it does say no, it has two ways to do it, and they are distinguishable from outside:

|                 | Reject                    | Drop                     |
| --------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Sends           | A TCP reset or ICMP error | Nothing at all           |
| Client sees     | An instant failure        | A timeout, after retries |
| Tells a scanner | “Something is here”       | Nothing                  |

That is the same distinction you met in track 2 as _fast failure versus slow failure_,
now from the other side of the boundary.
