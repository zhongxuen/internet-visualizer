# Messages travel in small packets

How does a message get from your laptop to a <Term>server</Term> in another country?

Nothing you send travels in one piece. It is cut into small <Term>packets</Term>. A packet is
like a parcel with a label. The label says where it came from and where it is going.

No single machine knows the whole way. Each <Term>router</Term> passes the packet one step
on, like a sorting office passing parcels on. Each step is called a <Term>hop</Term>.

Before your message is sent, your laptop and the server agree to talk. That opening exchange
is a <Term>handshake</Term>, and it is part of <Term>TCP</Term>. TCP makes sure every packet
arrives, like recorded delivery: every piece is signed for.

On the way out, the home router changes where the label says the packet came from. The
laptop's <Term>IP address</Term> only works inside its home. So the router swaps in the one address the whole home shares. It remembers the
swap, so the reply can find its way back to the laptop.

That swap is called <Term>NAT</Term>. It works like one street address for a whole
building, with a front desk that remembers who ordered what.
