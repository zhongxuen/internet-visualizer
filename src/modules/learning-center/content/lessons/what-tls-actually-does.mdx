# What TLS actually does

<Term>TLS</Term> gives three guarantees, and the most useful thing to know about them is
that they are separate:

| Guarantee       | Means                                         | Broken by                   |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Confidentiality | Nobody on the path can read the traffic       | A weak or leaked key        |
| Integrity       | Nobody can alter it without being detected    | A forged authentication tag |
| Authentication  | The far end is who the certificate says it is | A bad certificate check     |

HTTPS is exactly HTTP carried inside that. Not a different protocol — the methods,
status codes and fields from track 4 are byte-for-byte identical. Only the envelope
changed.

## Agreeing on a key in public

The strange part of that run is where the key comes from. Everything you watched crossed
a wire an attacker is reading in full, and at the end both sides share a secret the
attacker cannot reconstruct.

That is an ephemeral Diffie–Hellman exchange. Each side generates a temporary key pair,
sends the public half, and combines its own private half with the other's public half.
Both arrive at the same value; an observer who saw both public halves cannot.

The word _ephemeral_ is doing real work: the keys are thrown away when the connection
ends, which gives <Term>forward secrecy</Term>. Traffic recorded today stays unreadable
even to somebody who steals the server's long-term private key tomorrow.

## What survives the encryption

A great deal less is hidden than people assume. An observer on the path still learns:

- the IP address you connected to, because packets have to be routed;
- the hostname, in most deployments, from the SNI field in the ClientHello;
- the size and timing of every message, which is often enough to identify a page;
- that a connection happened at all, and for how long.

The body is confidential. The metadata around it largely is not — and that is a
structural property, not an implementation gap.
