# Why pages feel slow

Take the cold first visit from lesson 1 and change nothing about the page. Change only
the link underneath it — 200 ms of <Term>latency</Term> instead of 20, and a fraction of
the <Term>bandwidth</Term>.

<EmbeddedSim module="internet-simulator" scenario="slow-network" />

## Both halves went up. Only one of them mattered.

The bandwidth drop made the bodies take longer to transfer. That is linear, predictable,
and usually the smaller number. The latency increase multiplied every

<Term>round trip</Term> in the load — and a cold HTTPS page load contains four before the
HTML exists, plus one more for every resource discovered afterwards.

```text
Cost of one page load, roughly:

  (number of round trips  x  latency)   +   (total bytes / bandwidth)
   ^                                         ^
   grows with distance and protocol           grows with page weight
   design; unfixable by paying more           fixable by sending less
```

This is why a satellite link with more bandwidth than 3G still feels worse, and why
essentially every protocol change of the last decade — TLS 1.3's single round trip,
session resumption, QUIC folding the transport and TLS handshakes together — has been
an attack on the left-hand term.

## The other multiplier: how many requests queue

Latency sets the price of one round trip. The protocol decides how many of them you are
forced to make in sequence — and that is the part you can actually change.

<EmbeddedSim module="http-explorer" scenario="http2-multiplexing" />

Under HTTP/1.1 a browser opens about six connections per host and everything else waits
in line, so twenty-four resources become four rounds of queueing. HTTP/2 sends them all
at once over one connection and that queue disappears. What does not disappear is

<Term id="head-of-line-blocking">head-of-line blocking</Term> underneath: TCP still
delivers in order, so one lost segment stalls every stream sharing the connection.
