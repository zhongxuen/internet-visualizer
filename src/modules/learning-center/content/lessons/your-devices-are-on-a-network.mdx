# Your devices are on a network

What joins your laptop and your phone to each other, and to the rest of the world?

Your laptop, your phone and your TV are already on a <Term>network</Term>. A network is
devices joined together so they can send each other messages. Some join by cable. Others
join by <Term>Wi-Fi</Term>, which carries the same messages by radio.

Your laptop and your phone each have an <Term>IP address</Term>, a number that says where
they are. It works like a street address: messages are sent to it.

Three kinds of box hold the network together.

- An <Term>access point</Term> sends and receives the Wi-Fi.
- A <Term>switch</Term> passes messages between the devices plugged into it, like a
  building's internal mail room.
- A <Term>router</Term> passes messages from your network on to the next one, like a
  sorting office passing parcels on.

Only one device in the house sends messages on to another network: the router. The laptop
and the phone never need to know the way out. Anything meant for the outside world, they
hand to the router.

Past the router, the messages leave the house. The next router belongs
to your <Term>internet service provider</Term>, the company you pay for your connection.

From there, messages cross networks run by other companies. All of those networks, joined
together, are the <Term>internet</Term>.
